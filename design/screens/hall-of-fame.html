Hall of Fame

The distributions that shaped Linux itself. Curated editorially, with reasons and sources — not a popularity contest. For what's hot right now, see the rankings.

1992·1998·2004·2010·2016
S

Slackware

1993 — present
Inducted 2026

The oldest surviving distribution. Patrick Volkerding's insistence on simplicity and vanilla upstream software defined an entire philosophy of Linux — one still audible in every "keep it simple" argument today.

Sources: slackware.com · LWN retrospective (2023)

D

Debian

1993 — present
Inducted 2026

The universal operating system and the root of the largest family tree in Linux. Its Social Contract and rigorous packaging culture became the moral and technical backbone for hundreds of derivatives, Ubuntu included.

Sources: debian.org/social_contract · debian.org/history

RH

Red Hat Linux

1995 — 2004
Inducted 2026

Proved that free software could carry a business — and an enterprise. RPM, Anaconda, and the commercial support model it pioneered live on in RHEL, CentOS's successors, and Fedora.

Sources: redhat.com/history · Wikipedia (Red Hat Linux)

Discontinued
Md

Mandrake Linux

1998 — 2011
Inducted 2026

The first distribution that treated desktop usability as the product. Graphical installers, hardware detection, and friendly defaults that today feel obvious were Mandrake inventions first.

Sources: Wikipedia (Mandriva) · archive.org press coverage

Discontinued
G

Gentoo

2002 — present
Inducted 2026

Source-based, endlessly configurable, and the training ground for a generation of kernel and toolchain developers. Portage made "compile everything, your way" a coherent system rather than a stunt.

Sources: gentoo.org · Gentoo Wiki history

U

Ubuntu

2004 — present
Inducted 2026

"Linux for human beings" — the release that took desktop Linux from hobbyists to the mainstream, shipped free CDs worldwide, and set the six-month cadence much of the ecosystem still keeps time by.

Sources: ubuntu.com/about · Mark Shuttleworth announcement (2004)
